Chapter 1

Diagnostic/Descriptive AI: Assessing the Correctness of Behavior from Historical Data

Guiding questions: What happened? Why did it happen? Was the observed behavior correct? What evidence supports that judgment? What should be done next?

Abstract

Diagnostic/descriptive AI is the branch of artificial intelligence that analyzes historical data to reconstruct, summarize, and explain the behavior of systems, processes, organizations, and humans. Descriptive AI answers what happened. Diagnostic AI answers why it happened. When combined with specifications, norms, causal models, and domain knowledge, they also assess the correctness of behavior: whether an observed action, process, decision, or outcome conformed to expectations, rules, goals, or causal pathways.

This chapter presents diagnostic/descriptive AI as a general but deep learning–enabled subject for multiple expert arrays: machine learning engineers, data scientists, domain specialists, systems operators, causal inference researchers, human-factors experts, auditors, ethicists, and decision-makers. It covers formal definitions, data modalities, deep learning architectures, causal reasoning, evaluation, reference architectures, applications, case studies, challenges, and exercises.

1.1 Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter, readers should be able to:

  1. Distinguish descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive AI.
  2. Define “correctness of behavior” across functional, procedural, causal, normative, operational, and decision dimensions.
  3. Formulate descriptive and diagnostic problems over historical data.
  4. Select deep learning methods for logs, traces, time series, graphs, text, images, and multimodal records.
  5. Combine deep representation learning with causal inference and explainability.
  6. Design a reference architecture for diagnostic/descriptive AI in high-stakes environments.
  7. Evaluate diagnostic systems using technical, causal, human, and governance criteria.
  8. Recognize pitfalls such as correlation–causation confusion, data drift, label scarcity, and LLM hallucination.

1.2 Introduction: Why Diagnostic/Descriptive AI Matters

Modern digital and physical systems generate enormous historical records: application logs, metrics, distributed traces, sensor streams, transaction records, clinical notes, manufacturing events, process logs, images, audio, and human decisions. These records describe what happened, but raw data alone does not explain why it happened or whether it was correct.

Descriptive AI transforms historical data into summaries, patterns, anomalies, clusters, timelines, and process models. Diagnostic AI goes further by inferring causes, localizing faults, attributing responsibility, and generating counterfactual explanations. In high-stakes domains—healthcare, finance, cybersecurity, manufacturing, autonomous systems, IT operations, and public administration—the critical question is not only “What happened?” but “Was this behavior correct, and why or why not?”

Example. A microservice latency spike appears in metrics. Descriptive AI reports: latency increased 400% at 14:03. Diagnostic AI infers: a configuration change at 13:58 reduced the database connection pool, causing queue buildup in service A, which propagated to service B. Correctness assessment asks: Did the deployment follow policy? Was the SLO violated? Was the operator’s response correct? Was the root cause causal or merely correlated?

Thus, diagnostic/descriptive AI is not merely retrospective analytics. It is an evidence-based reasoning system for accountability, learning, and improvement.

1.3 Core Definitions and Scope

1.3.1 Behavior

Behavior is the observable sequence of states, actions, events, and outcomes produced by a system, process, human, or organization over time.

Examples:

  • A microservice handling a request.
  • A clinician following a treatment pathway.
  • A financial trading algorithm executing orders.
  • A manufacturing robot assembling a component.
  • A business process approving a loan.

1.3.2 Historical Data

Historical data includes:

  • Time series: metrics, sensor readings, KPIs.
  • Event logs: system logs, audit trails, process logs.
  • Traces: distributed request paths.
  • Text: incident reports, clinical notes, tickets.
  • Images/video/audio: visual inspection, surveillance, speech.
  • Graphs: service topologies, social networks, supply chains, causal graphs.
  • Human decisions: approvals, overrides, annotations.

1.3.3 Descriptive AI

Descriptive AI answers: What happened?

Tasks:

  • Summarization and timeline reconstruction.
  • Clustering and segmentation.
  • Change-point and drift detection.
  • Process discovery and conformance checking.
  • Anomaly detection.
  • Pattern mining.

1.3.4 Diagnostic AI

Diagnostic AI answers: Why did it happen?

Tasks:

  • Root cause analysis.
  • Fault localization.
  • Attribution and blame assignment.
  • Counterfactual reasoning.
  • Causal discovery and effect estimation.
  • Hypothesis generation and ranking.

1.3.5 Correctness of Behavior

Correctness is the degree to which observed behavior conforms to:

  • Functional specifications: outputs meet requirements.
  • Procedural rules: steps follow policies, protocols, or workflows.
  • Causal expectations: actions produced intended effects through valid mechanisms.
  • Normative standards: legal, ethical, safety, or fairness norms.
  • Operational objectives: reliability, latency, cost, throughput, safety.
  • Decision quality: expected utility under uncertainty.

Correctness can be binary, graded, context-dependent, or contested. Diagnostic AI must represent uncertainty and multiple expert perspectives.

1.5 Formal Problem Formulation

Let historical data be represented as:

\[ \mathcal{D} = \{(x_t, a_t, y_t, e_t, g_t)\}_{t=1}^{T} \]

where \(x_t\): observations at time \(t\), \(a_t\): actions or interventions, \(y_t\): outcomes, \(e_t\): events or logs, \(g_t\): contextual graph or relational structure.

1.5.1 Descriptive Problem

Learn a function:

\[ f_{\text{desc}}: \mathcal{D} \rightarrow \mathcal{S} \]

where \(\mathcal{S}\) is a set of summaries, segments, anomalies, clusters, or process models.

1.5.2 Diagnostic Problem

Infer a posterior over causes:

\[ P(c \mid \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{K}) \]

where \(c\) is a cause or set of causes, and \(\mathcal{K}\) is background knowledge such as topology, policies, or causal graphs.

1.5.3 Correctness Problem

Given a specification or expectation model \(\mathcal{M}\), compute:

\[ C(\mathcal{D}) = \text{score}(\text{observed behavior}, \text{expected behavior under } \mathcal{M}) \]

This may involve conformance checking, anomaly scoring, causal attribution, counterfactual comparison, and normative evaluation.

1.5.4 Levels of Diagnostic Output

  1. Detection: Something is wrong.
  2. Localization: Where is it wrong?
  3. Attribution: What caused it?
  4. Explanation: Why did it cause it?
  5. Counterfactual: What would have happened otherwise?
  6. Actionability: What should be done?

1.6 Data Modalities and Preparation

Diagnostic/descriptive AI must handle heterogeneous data:

  • Time series: metrics, sensors, financial prices.
  • Logs: unstructured or semi-structured text.
  • Traces: distributed request graphs.
  • Events: process logs, audit trails.
  • Text: tickets, reports, clinical notes.
  • Images/video: inspection, surveillance.
  • Graphs: service dependencies, social networks, causal graphs.
  • Human decisions: approvals, overrides, annotations.

Key challenges:

  • Missing data and irregular sampling.
  • Class imbalance and rare events.
  • Label scarcity and noisy labels.
  • Temporal leakage.
  • Concept drift and nonstationarity.
  • Privacy, security, and access control.

Deep learning can learn representations directly from raw data, but preprocessing, alignment, and domain validation remain essential.

1.7 Deep Learning Methods

1.7.1 Representation Learning

Autoencoders, variational autoencoders (VAEs), and contrastive learning learn compact latent representations.

Autoencoder loss:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \|x - \hat{x}\|^2 + \lambda \Omega(z) \]

where \(z\) is the latent code and \(\Omega\) is a regularizer.

Uses: dimensionality reduction, denoising, anomaly detection via reconstruction error, pretraining for downstream diagnosis.

1.7.2 Sequence and Temporal Models

  • RNNs/LSTMs/GRUs: sequential dependence, variable-length logs.
  • Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs): long-range temporal patterns.
  • Transformers: attention over long sequences, multimodal fusion.

Self-supervised objectives: next-event prediction, masked token modeling, contrastive temporal prediction.

Applications: log anomaly detection, incident prediction, clinical pathway analysis, process deviation detection.

1.7.3 Graph and Relational Models

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) model dependencies:

\[ h_v^{(k+1)} = \text{AGG}\left(\{h_u^{(k)} : u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\}\right) \]

Uses: root cause localization in microservice graphs, fault propagation in supply chains, causal graph learning, social network diagnostics.

1.7.4 Multimodal Fusion

Fusion strategies: early fusion (concatenate raw features), late fusion (combine model outputs), cross-attention (align modalities).

Example: combine logs, metrics, traces, and deployment events for incident diagnosis.

1.7.5 Anomaly and Correctness Detection

Methods:

  • Reconstruction-based: autoencoders, VAEs.
  • Forecasting-based: predict next values, measure residual.
  • Density-based: estimate \(p(x)\), flag low-probability points.
  • One-class classification: Deep SVDD.
  • Energy-based models.

Anomaly score:

\[ s(x) = -\log p_\theta(x) \]

Correctness extends anomaly detection by comparing against specifications and norms.

1.7.6 Root Cause and Causal Diagnosis

Causal reasoning is essential. Deep learning alone finds correlations. Causal inference provides identification, interventions, and counterfactuals.

Structural Causal Model (SCM):

\[ \mathcal{M} = (U, V, F) \]

Intervention: \(do(X = x)\). Counterfactual: \(Y_x(u)\).

Methods: causal discovery, invariant learning, disentangled representations, counterfactual explanation, graph attention for root cause ranking.

Root cause ranking:

\[ \hat{c} = \arg\max_c P(c \mid \mathcal{D}, G, \mathcal{K}) \]

1.7.7 Explanation and Human Interaction

Explainability methods: SHAP, Integrated Gradients, attention visualization, concept bottleneck models, prototypes and counterfactual explanations, LLM-generated narratives with citations and uncertainty.

Human-in-the-loop: domain expert validation, active learning, feedback correction.

1.7.8 LLMs and Agents

Large language models can summarize incidents, generate hypotheses, query tools and logs, and draft reports.

Risks: hallucination, lack of grounding, overconfidence, privacy leakage.

Mitigations: retrieval-augmented generation, tool use and verification, uncertainty quantification, human review.

1.8 Reference Architecture

A general diagnostic/descriptive AI architecture includes:

  1. Data ingestion: logs, metrics, traces, events, text, images.
  2. Storage and governance: data lake, access control, lineage.
  3. Representation layer: feature extraction, embeddings, multimodal fusion.
  4. Descriptive engine: summarization, clustering, anomaly detection, process mining.
  5. Diagnostic engine: root cause analysis, causal inference, counterfactuals.
  6. Correctness evaluator: conformance, norms, SLOs, policies.
  7. Explanation layer: visualizations, narratives, evidence.
  8. Human-in-the-loop: review, feedback, active learning.
  9. Action layer: alerts, recommendations, automation.
  10. Governance: auditing, fairness, privacy, security.

1.11 Case Study: Microservice Incident Diagnosis

Scenario. An e-commerce platform experiences a 400% latency increase.

Data. Metrics (CPU, memory, latency, queue length); logs (application errors, config changes); traces (request paths across services); graph (service dependency topology); events (deployment, rollback, alerts).

Descriptive AI.

  • Detects latency spike at 14:03.
  • Identifies affected services: A, B, C.
  • Reconstructs timeline: config change at 13:58 → connection pool reduction → queue buildup → latency propagation.

Diagnostic AI.

  • Transformer encodes logs and metrics.
  • GNN propagates anomalies over service graph.
  • Causal layer infers: config change caused connection pool exhaustion.
  • Counterfactual: without config change, latency would remain normal.

Correctness Assessment.

  • SLO violated: latency > 200 ms for 10 minutes.
  • Policy nonconformance: config change lacked required review.
  • Operator response: rollback at 14:15 was correct but delayed.

Output. What happened: latency spike. Why: config change reduced connection pool. Was it correct: no; SLO and policy violated. Evidence: logs, metrics, traces, deployment records. Action: automate config validation and rollback.

1.12 Challenges and Open Problems

  1. Correlation vs. causation: Deep models find patterns, not causes.
  2. Nonstationarity: Behavior changes over time.
  3. Rare events: Few examples of failures.
  4. Label scarcity: Ground truth is expensive.
  5. Uncertainty: Models must know what they do not know.
  6. Explainability: Explanations must be faithful and actionable.
  7. Privacy and security: Sensitive historical data.
  8. Adversarial manipulation: Data poisoning and evasion.
  9. Human factors: Trust, alert fatigue, automation bias.
  10. Governance: Accountability, fairness, auditability.
  11. LLM hallucination: Grounding and verification.
  12. Multi-expert conflict: Different experts may disagree on correctness.

1.13 Summary

Diagnostic/descriptive AI analyzes historical data to understand what happened and why. When combined with specifications, norms, and causal models, it assesses the correctness of behavior. Deep learning provides powerful representation, sequence, graph, multimodal, and generative tools. Causal inference provides identification and counterfactual reasoning. Human experts provide semantics, validation, and accountability. A successful system is multi-expert, multi-modal, causal, explainable, and governed.

Key takeaway: Diagnostic/descriptive AI is not just retrospective reporting. It is an evidence-based reasoning infrastructure for correctness, accountability, and improvement.

Chapter 2

Predictive AI: Forecasting Future Outcomes from Historical and Current Data

Guiding questions: What will happen? When will it happen? How likely is it? Under what conditions? How confident is the forecast? What decisions should it inform? How do we know the prediction is correct?

Abstract

Predictive AI is the branch of artificial intelligence concerned with forecasting future outcomes based on historical and current data. If descriptive AI asks what happened and diagnostic AI asks why it happened, predictive AI asks what is likely to happen next. It produces point forecasts, probability distributions, event probabilities, risk scores, trajectories, and scenario-dependent predictions.

This chapter presents predictive AI as a general but deep learning–enabled subject for multiple expert arrays: machine learning engineers, data scientists, statisticians, causal inference researchers, domain specialists, systems operators, human-factors experts, ethicists, auditors, and decision-makers. It covers definitions, formal problem formulations, data modalities, deep learning architectures, probabilistic forecasting, uncertainty quantification, causal prediction, evaluation, reference architectures, applications, case studies, challenges, and exercises.

2.1 Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter, readers should be able to:

  1. Distinguish predictive AI from descriptive, diagnostic, and prescriptive AI.
  2. Define forecasting problems across horizons, targets, and uncertainty types.
  3. Formulate point, probabilistic, event-based, and decision-theoretic predictions.
  4. Select deep learning architectures for time series, events, graphs, text, images, and multimodal data.
  5. Quantify aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty using Bayesian, ensemble, quantile, and conformal methods.
  6. Integrate causal reasoning into forecasting under interventions and counterfactuals.
  7. Design a reference architecture for predictive AI in high-stakes environments.
  8. Evaluate predictions using accuracy, calibration, sharpness, decision utility, fairness, and robustness.
  9. Recognize pitfalls such as leakage, nonstationarity, feedback loops, and LLM hallucination.

2.2 Introduction: Why Predictive AI Matters

Organizations, systems, and individuals constantly need to anticipate the future. Demand must be forecast, machines must be maintained before failure, patients must be monitored before deterioration, fraud must be detected before loss, and resources must be allocated before scarcity. Predictive AI transforms historical and current data into forward-looking estimates.

Example. A hospital wants to predict which patients will deteriorate within 24 hours. Descriptive AI reports current vital signs and historical trends. Diagnostic AI explains why a patient is currently unstable. Predictive AI estimates the probability of deterioration, the likely time window, and the uncertainty around that estimate. Prescriptive AI then recommends an intervention.

Predictive AI is not merely extrapolation. It must handle:

  • Nonstationarity and distribution shift.
  • Rare and extreme events.
  • Long and short horizons.
  • Multiple interacting entities.
  • Feedback loops from decisions.
  • Uncertainty that is irreducible or knowledge-based.
  • Human trust and decision-making.

A prediction is only useful if it is accurate, calibrated, timely, actionable, and trustworthy.

2.3 Core Definitions and Scope

2.3.1 Prediction, Forecast, and Nowcast

  • Prediction: A general statement about an unknown outcome, past, present, or future.
  • Forecast: A prediction specifically about future values or events.
  • Nowcast: Estimation of the present state when data are delayed or incomplete.

2.3.2 Forecasting Horizon

  • Short-term: seconds to hours.
  • Medium-term: days to weeks.
  • Long-term: months to years.

Horizon affects model choice, uncertainty, and feature availability.

2.3.3 Prediction Targets

  • Point forecast: single value, e.g., next-day demand.
  • Interval forecast: range, e.g., 90% prediction interval.
  • Probabilistic forecast: full distribution.
  • Event forecast: probability of an event, e.g., failure, fraud, relapse.
  • Trajectory forecast: sequence of future states.
  • Hierarchical forecast: predictions across aggregated and disaggregated levels.
  • Spatiotemporal forecast: predictions over space and time.

2.3.4 Historical and Current Data

Predictive AI uses historical time series, current observations, exogenous covariates, events and logs, text, images, audio, video, graph and relational data, human decisions and annotations, and calendar, weather, economic, and policy signals.

2.3.5 Predictive Correctness

A prediction is correct when it is:

  • Accurate: close to observed outcomes.
  • Calibrated: stated probabilities match observed frequencies.
  • Sharp: confident when appropriate.
  • Timely: available before decisions must be made.
  • Robust: stable under drift and noise.
  • Decision-useful: improves expected utility.
  • Fair: does not systematically harm groups.
  • Explainable: provides actionable reasons.

Correctness is therefore multi-dimensional and context-dependent.

2.5 Formal Problem Formulation

Let historical and current data be:

\[ \mathcal{D} = \{(x_{1:t}, y_{t+1:t+H}, u_{1:t}, g_t)\} \]

where \(x_{1:t}\): observations up to time \(t\), \(y_{t+1:t+H}\): future target values over horizon \(H\), \(u_{1:t}\): interventions or actions, \(g_t\): graph or relational context.

2.5.1 Point Forecasting

\[ \hat{y}_{t+1:t+H} = f_\theta(x_{1:t}, u_{1:t}, g_t) \]

2.5.2 Probabilistic Forecasting

\[ p_\theta(y_{t+1:t+H} \mid x_{1:t}, u_{1:t}, g_t, \mathcal{K}) \]

where \(\mathcal{K}\) is background knowledge.

2.5.3 Event Forecasting

\[ P(\text{event occurs in } [t+1, t+H] \mid x_{1:t}, \mathcal{K}) \]

2.5.4 Quantile Forecasting

\[ q_\tau(x) = \inf\{y : P(Y \leq y \mid x) \geq \tau\} \]

2.5.5 Decision-Theoretic Forecasting

Choose action \(a\) to minimize expected loss:

\[ a^* = \arg\min_a \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p(y|x)}[L(y, a)] \]

2.5.6 Causal Forecasting

Predict under intervention:

\[ P(y_{t+1:t+H} \mid do(a), x_{1:t}, \mathcal{K}) \]

and counterfactual:

\[ P(y_{t+1:t+H}^{a'} \mid x_{1:t}, \mathcal{K}) \]

2.5.7 Levels of Predictive Output

  1. Detection: Will something happen?
  2. Timing: When will it happen?
  3. Magnitude: How large will it be?
  4. Uncertainty: How confident are we?
  5. Scenario: What if conditions change?
  6. Actionability: What decision does it support?

2.6 Data Modalities and Preparation

Predictive AI handles heterogeneous data:

  • Time series: sensors, metrics, prices, demand.
  • Events: logs, transactions, clicks, admissions.
  • Text: reports, news, clinical notes, tickets.
  • Images/video: inspection, surveillance, remote sensing.
  • Audio: speech, machine sounds, acoustic monitoring.
  • Graphs: supply chains, social networks, service topologies.
  • Exogenous variables: weather, holidays, economic indicators.
  • Human decisions: actions, overrides, interventions.

Key challenges:

  • Missing data and irregular sampling.
  • Temporal leakage.
  • Concept drift and nonstationarity.
  • Rare events and class imbalance.
  • Feature availability at prediction time.
  • Feedback loops from past decisions.
  • Privacy and security.

Proper backtesting, rolling-origin evaluation, and time-based splits are essential.

2.7 Deep Learning Methods

2.7.1 Sequence and Temporal Models

  • RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs: sequential dependence.
  • Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs): long-range patterns.
  • Transformers: attention over long sequences and multimodal inputs.
  • State-space models: efficient long-horizon forecasting.

Example attention:

\[ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right)V \]

2.7.2 Probabilistic Deep Forecasting

  • DeepAR: autoregressive probabilistic forecasting.
  • Normalizing flows: flexible distributions.
  • VAEs: latent variable models.
  • Diffusion models: generative forecasting.
  • Quantile regression: direct quantile estimation.
  • Distributional regression: predict parameters of a distribution.

Loss examples: Gaussian negative log-likelihood, quantile loss (pinball loss), continuous ranked probability score (CRPS).

2.7.3 Graph and Spatiotemporal Models

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs):

\[ h_v^{(k+1)} = \text{AGG}\left(\{h_u^{(k)} : u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\}\right) \]

Uses: traffic forecasting, supply chain demand, energy grid load, epidemic spread, financial contagion.

2.7.4 Multimodal Fusion

Fusion strategies: early fusion (concatenate features), late fusion (combine outputs), cross-attention (align modalities).

Example: combine time series, text news, and graph relations for financial forecasting.

2.7.5 Foundation Models and LLMs

  • Time series foundation models: zero-shot and few-shot forecasting.
  • LLMs: narrative forecasts, scenario generation, tool use.
  • Retrieval-augmented generation: ground forecasts in evidence.
  • Agents: query data, run models, produce reports.

Risks: hallucination, overconfidence, lack of numerical grounding, privacy leakage.

Mitigations: tool verification, uncertainty quantification, human review, hybrid statistical and neural models.

2.7.6 Uncertainty Quantification

  • Aleatoric uncertainty: inherent noise.
  • Epistemic uncertainty: model ignorance.
  • Bayesian neural networks: posterior over weights.
  • MC dropout: approximate Bayesian inference.
  • Deep ensembles: robust uncertainty.
  • Conformal prediction: distribution-free intervals.
  • Evidential deep learning: predict evidence parameters.

2.7.7 Causal and Hybrid Forecasting

Prescriptive AI: Determining the Optimal Course of Action

Guiding questions: What should be done? Which action maximizes expected utility? Under what constraints? For whom? With what trade-offs? How do we evaluate a recommendation before acting? How do we ensure safety, fairness, and accountability?

Abstract

Prescriptive AI is the branch of artificial intelligence that determines the optimal course of action by providing recommendations based on data analysis. If descriptive AI asks what happened, diagnostic AI asks why it happened, and predictive AI asks what will happen, prescriptive AI asks what should be done. It combines prediction, causal inference, optimization, decision theory, and human judgment to recommend actions, policies, or interventions.

This chapter presents prescriptive AI as a general but deep learning–enabled subject for multiple expert arrays: operations researchers, machine learning engineers, economists, causal inference researchers, domain specialists, human-factors experts, ethicists, auditors, and decision-makers. It covers formal decision frameworks, optimization methods, reinforcement learning, causal prescriptive analysis, multi-objective trade-offs, fairness, evaluation, reference architectures, applications, case studies, challenges, and exercises.

3.1 Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter, readers should be able to:

  1. Distinguish prescriptive AI from descriptive, diagnostic, and predictive AI.
  2. Formulate decision problems using utility theory, constraints, and uncertainty.
  3. Apply optimization, reinforcement learning, and causal inference to recommend actions.
  4. Handle multi-objective trade-offs, fairness, and risk.
  5. Design a reference architecture for prescriptive AI in high-stakes environments.
  6. Evaluate recommendations using off-policy evaluation, regret, utility, and safety metrics.
  7. Recognize pitfalls such as confounding, reward hacking, feedback loops, and unsafe exploration.
  8. Integrate human-in-the-loop oversight and governance.

3.2 Introduction: Why Prescriptive AI Matters

Prediction alone does not improve outcomes. Knowing that a machine will fail, a patient will deteriorate, or demand will spike is useful only if we act appropriately. Prescriptive AI closes the loop from insight to action. It recommends treatments, maintenance schedules, prices, routes, resource allocations, marketing campaigns, policies, and interventions.

Example. A hospital predicts that a patient has a 30% chance of deterioration within 24 hours. Prescriptive AI recommends: increase monitoring frequency, administer a specific medication, and prepare an ICU bed. The recommendation balances expected survival benefit, side effects, cost, bed availability, and ethical considerations.

Prescriptive AI is inherently interdisciplinary. It requires:

  • A model of the world (prediction and causality).
  • A model of preferences (utility, costs, constraints).
  • A model of uncertainty (probabilities, risk).
  • A model of action (feasible interventions).
  • A model of humans (trust, adoption, feedback).
  • A model of governance (accountability, fairness, safety).

3.3 Core Definitions and Scope

3.3.1 Recommendation, Decision, and Policy

  • Recommendation: A suggested action for a specific situation.
  • Decision: The choice among alternatives, possibly by a human or system.
  • Policy: A mapping from states to actions, applied repeatedly.

3.3.2 Utility, Cost, and Risk

  • Utility: A measure of preference or value.
  • Cost: Negative utility, including monetary, health, and social costs.
  • Risk: Uncertainty about outcomes, often measured by variance, VaR, or CVaR.

3.3.3 Constraints

  • Hard constraints: must be satisfied (safety, legality, budgets).
  • Soft constraints: preferred but can be violated at a cost.
  • Resource constraints: time, money, staff, capacity.

3.3.4 Prescriptive Correctness

A recommendation is correct when it:

  • Maximizes expected utility under the stated model.
  • Respects constraints and norms.
  • Is robust to model uncertainty and distribution shift.
  • Is fair across groups and individuals.
  • Is safe under exploration and error.
  • Is explainable and auditable.
  • Is adopted and actionable by humans.

3.5 Formal Problem Formulation

3.5.1 Decision-Theoretic Formulation

Given state \(s\), action \(a\), outcome \(y\), and utility \(U(y,a)\), choose:

\[ a^* = \arg\max_a \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p(y \mid s, a)}[U(y, a)] \]

3.5.2 Constrained Optimization

\[ \max_a \mathbb{E}[U(y,a)] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad g_i(a) \leq 0, \; h_j(a) = 0 \]

3.5.3 Markov Decision Process (MDP)

\[ \mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma) \]

Policy:

\[ \pi^* = \arg\max_\pi \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t R(s_t, a_t)\right] \]

3.5.4 Partially Observable MDP (POMDP)

When states are not fully observed, maintain a belief \(b(s)\) and optimize over belief space.

3.5.5 Causal Prescriptive Formulation

Choose action to maximize expected outcome under intervention:

\[ a^* = \arg\max_a \mathbb{E}[Y \mid do(A=a), X=x] \]

3.5.6 Multi-Objective Optimization

\[ \max_a \left( f_1(a), f_2(a), \ldots, f_k(a) \right) \]

Solutions form a Pareto frontier. Choose based on preferences or constraints.

3.5.7 Levels of Prescriptive Output

  1. Ranking: order actions by expected value.
  2. Recommendation: best action under model.
  3. Policy: rule mapping states to actions.
  4. Plan: sequence of actions over time.
  5. Adaptive policy: learns and updates with feedback.
  6. Governed policy: includes safety, fairness, and audit.

3.7 Methods

3.7.1 Optimization and Operations Research

  • Linear programming (LP): continuous decision variables, linear objectives.
  • Mixed-integer programming (MIP): discrete decisions.
  • Convex optimization: tractable global optima.
  • Dynamic programming: sequential decisions.
  • Stochastic programming: uncertainty in parameters.
  • Robust optimization: worst-case guarantees.

3.7.2 Reinforcement Learning

  • Value-based: Q-learning, DQN.
  • Policy-based: REINFORCE, PPO, TRPO.
  • Actor-critic: A2C, A3C, SAC, TD3.
  • Model-based: learn dynamics, plan.
  • Offline RL: learn from logged data without exploration.
  • Multi-agent RL: interacting agents.

3.7.3 Contextual Bandits

\[ a_t = \arg\max_a \left( \hat{\mu}_a(x_t) + \beta \cdot \text{UCB}_a(x_t) \right) \]

Abstract

Reactive AI is designed to respond to specific inputs with predetermined responses. It does not maintain memory of past interactions, does not learn from experience, and does not model the world beyond the current stimulus. Reactive AI is the simplest and oldest form of AI, but it remains foundational in control systems, games, user interfaces, and embedded devices.

5.2 Introduction: The Simplest Intelligence

Reactive AI maps inputs directly to outputs. A thermostat turns on the heater when temperature drops below a threshold. A spam filter classifies an email as spam or not. A game NPC attacks when the player enters a radius. A chatbot returns a canned response to a keyword.

Example. A traffic light controller cycles through states based on timers and sensor inputs. It does not remember past traffic patterns or learn from them. It reacts.

Reactive AI is not “dumb.” It is appropriate when the environment is well-defined, responses are known, and memory or learning is unnecessary or unsafe.

5.3 Core Definitions and Scope

5.3.2 Characteristics of Reactive AI

  • No memory of past interactions.
  • No learning from experience.
  • No internal world model.
  • Fast, predictable, deterministic (often).
  • Brittle outside design conditions.

5.3.3 Reactive Correctness

A reactive system is correct when:

  • It responds correctly to all specified inputs.
  • It is robust to noise and edge cases.
  • It fails safely when inputs are out of distribution.
  • It is efficient and real-time capable.
  • It is transparent and auditable.

Narrow AI (Weak AI): Designed to Perform Specific Tasks

Guiding questions: What is narrow AI? How does it differ from general AI? Why is all deployed AI today narrow? What are its strengths, limits, and risks? How do we design, evaluate, and govern narrow AI systems responsibly?

Abstract

Narrow AI — also called weak AI — is artificial intelligence designed to perform a specific task or a narrow set of tasks. Unlike hypothetical Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), narrow AI does not possess general reasoning, transferable understanding, or self-awareness. It excels within a bounded domain: recommending products, transcribing speech, detecting fraud, translating languages, generating images, playing Go, or driving a car under defined conditions.

This chapter presents narrow AI as the operational reality of AI today. Every deployed AI system — from your phone’s keyboard to hospital diagnostic tools to autonomous vehicles — is narrow. Understanding narrow AI is therefore not an academic exercise; it is the foundation of professional AI literacy. The chapter covers definitions, task taxonomies, architectural patterns, evaluation, limitations, the path toward generality, governance, applications, case studies, and exercises.

9.1 Learning Objectives

9.2 Introduction: The AI We Actually Have

Headlines promise human-level AI, but the systems we use daily are narrow. A chess engine cannot hold a conversation. A recommender cannot diagnose disease. A chatbot cannot drive. Each is optimized for a specific task, with a specific data distribution, evaluated by specific metrics.

Key insight. Narrow AI is not “lesser” AI. It is the AI that creates value today — and it will continue to dominate for the foreseeable future. Mastering narrow AI means mastering scope, data, evaluation, and deployment.

Narrow AI is characterized by:

  • Bounded scope: one task or a small task family.
  • Task-specific data: trained and evaluated on a defined distribution.
  • Specialized metrics: accuracy, F1, BLEU, mAP, latency, cost.
  • Limited transfer: performance often drops outside the training distribution.
  • No general reasoning: no cross-domain understanding.
  • No consciousness or self-awareness: no subjective experience.

9.7 Limitations and Risks

  1. Brittleness: small distribution shifts cause large errors.
  2. Spurious correlations: models latch onto shortcuts.
  3. Bias: historical data encodes historical prejudice.
  4. Over-trust: users defer to confident but wrong outputs.
  5. Drift: the world changes; performance decays.
  6. Scope creep: using a narrow model outside its domain.
  7. Feedback loops: predictions shape future data.
  8. Adversarial vulnerability: small perturbations cause failures.
  9. Lack of common sense: no world model.
  10. Accountability gaps: who is responsible when it fails?
Warning. The most common failure mode of narrow AI is not the model — it is misuse outside its intended scope. Always document the intended use, out-of-scope uses, and failure modes.

9.8 From Narrow to General: The Realistic Path

AGI is not a single breakthrough; it is a research program. Current progress suggests a gradual path:

  • Multi-task models: one model, many tasks (e.g., GPT-4, Gemini).
  • Foundation models: broad pretraining, task fine-tuning.
  • Tool use: models calling APIs, databases, and other models.
  • Agents: planning, memory, reflection, and action.
  • World models: learned simulators for reasoning.
  • Continual learning: learning without forgetting.
  • Meta-learning: learning to learn.

Even multi-task foundation models remain narrow in a deep sense: they lack robust common sense, grounded understanding, and reliable cross-domain reasoning. The gap to AGI is real and unresolved.

9.10 Case Study: Diabetic Retinopathy Screening

Scenario. A narrow AI system screens retinal images for diabetic retinopathy.

Scope. Binary classification: referable vs. non-referable DR. Intended use: screening, not diagnosis. Population: adults with diabetes, images from approved cameras.

Data. 100k labeled retinal images, expert adjudicated. Stratified by age, sex, ethnicity, camera type.

Model. CNN ensemble with calibration and uncertainty estimation.

Evaluation. Sensitivity 92%, specificity 88%, AUC 0.96. Calibration ECE 0.03. Subgroup AUC 0.94–0.97.

Deployment. Human-in-the-loop: AI triages, ophthalmologist confirms. Failsafe: uncertain cases escalated.

Governance. FDA clearance, post-market monitoring, drift detection, incident reporting.

Limitations. Not validated for other eye diseases, pediatric patients, or unapproved cameras. Performance drops on low-quality images.

AGI remains hypothetical; narrow AI is the professional reality.

Key takeaway. The future of AI is not only about building general intelligence — it is about deploying narrow intelligence responsibly, at scale, and with measurable impact.

11.4.2 Cost Modeling

Total Cost of Ownership (TCO):

\[ \text{TCO} = C_{\text{API}} + C_{\text{infra}} + C_{\text{integration}} + C_{\text{training}} + C_{\text{ops}} + C_{\text{risk}} \]

Example API cost calculation:

Monthly cost = (input_tokens × price_in + output_tokens × price_out) × requests
             = (2,000 × $0.003 + 500 × $0.015) / 1000 × 100,000
             = ($6 + $7.5) × 100
             = $1,350 / month

11.4.3 Energy and Sustainability

  • Inference energy per request.
  • Carbon intensity of data center region.
  • Model size vs. accuracy trade-off.
  • Quantization, distillation, pruning.

11.4.4 Efficiency Levers

  • Model choice: small models for simple tasks.
  • Caching: reuse frequent responses.
  • Batching: amortize overhead.
  • Quantization: 8-bit / 4-bit inference.
  • Distillation: small model mimics large.
  • Routing: send easy queries to cheap models.
  • Prompt optimization: shorter prompts, fewer tokens.
  • Streaming: improve perceived latency.

11.5 ROI Analysis

Return on Investment:

\[ \text{ROI} = \frac{\text{Value Gained} - \text{Total Cost}}{\text{Total Cost}} \times 100\% \]

Value sources:

  • Time saved: hours × loaded hourly cost.
  • Quality improvement: fewer errors, better outcomes.
  • Revenue uplift: conversions, retention.
  • Risk reduction: avoided incidents, compliance.
  • Scale: more output without more headcount.
Example ROI. A support team of 20 agents handles 10,000 tickets/month. AI triage reduces handle time by 25%. At $30/hour loaded, savings = 20 × 160 hrs × 0.25 × $30 = $24,000/month. AI cost = $2,000/month. ROI = ($24,000 − $2,000) / $2,000 = 1,100%.

11.6 Evaluation Protocol

A rigorous protocol:

  1. Define task and success criteria before evaluating.
  2. Build a gold-standard test set from your domain.
  3. Choose metrics aligned with business value.
  4. Run all candidate tools on the same test set.
  5. Blind evaluation where possible (human raters).
  6. Measure efficiency (latency, cost, throughput).
  7. Test edge cases and adversarial inputs.
  8. Check fairness across subgroups.
  9. Pilot in production with shadow mode.
  10. Monitor continuously after deployment.

P.8 Closing Reflection

AI is not a single technology but a spectrum of capabilities, from reactive systems to speculative self-awareness. The professional skill is not memorizing tools — it is understanding trade-offs, evaluating rigorously, and governing responsibly.

Final takeaway. The goal of AI literacy is not to know everything — it is to ask the right questions, measure honestly, and act with accountability.
